Station 1

Beat the line

Each dot is a house: its size across, its sale price up. Move the two sliders until the line runs through the middle of the cloud. The red lines are your misses (residuals). Can you get your RMSE as low as least squares?

price ≈ starting price + price per sq ft × size
Your score · 1,168 houses
The twist. Least squares makes the squared misses as small as possible, so nobody beats it on RMSE. But on MAE (the average miss) a slightly different line wins, because least squares listens extra hard to the big misses.
Station 2

The memorizer & the tax

Now the model gets only 100 houses but knobs (one coefficient per column), so it can memorize them. Ridge puts a tax on big coefficients; alpha is the tax rate. Slide it and watch two errors: on the 100 practice houses, and on 292 new houses. Where is the exam error lowest?

Read the U. Left: no tax, the model memorizes (practice error near zero, exam error huge). Right: so much tax that every coefficient is squashed and the model just guesses the average. The bottom of the U is the sweet spot, and cross-validation finds it using only the training houses.

One step = a typical difference between houses (one standard deviation) for a number, or having the category for a yes/no column. Price effect = percent change in the predicted price, holding everything else fixed. A pattern in past sales, not a cause.
Station 4

Price a house

You are the pricing analyst. Describe a house and the 21-feature lasso (the realtor's checklist) prices it, with an honest range and a receipt that says where the price comes from. Anything you don't set stays at a typical Ames house. Then build a house the model has never seen, and watch it warn you.

The model's price

8 in 10 test houses sold within this range of the model's price. A price without a range is a bet.

The model works in percentages (it was trained on log price), so one more quality point adds more dollars to a big house than to a small one. The receipt adds the features in the order listed; a different order would split the dollars a little differently, but the total stays the same.
Data: the Ames Housing data (Dean De Cock, 2011), the 1,460 sales of the Kaggle House Prices competition, split 80/20 with random_state=835 exactly as in the Session 6 notebook. Station 1 scores the training houses; Stations 2–4 score the 292 test houses. Station 2 uses ridge on raw dollars like the notebook's 100-house demo; Stations 3–4 use lasso on log price. Prices in Station 4 hold the other features at the training median (numbers) or the most common value (categories).

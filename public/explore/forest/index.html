STATION 1

Be the tree: find the best first question

Each dot is a booking: red canceled, teal stayed. A tree's first question cuts the map in two with one straight line. Drag the line (or use the slider) to make the two boxes as pure as you can. Purity is measured by Gini: the chance that two random guests from a box disagree. Then let the computer hold its audition and see who wins.

What the computer just did: it tried every cut on both clues (hundreds of candidate questions) and kept the one with the lowest Gini afterwards. That's the whole secret of a decision tree. Then it repeats the audition inside each box: greedy, one question at a time.
STATION 2

Too many questions: watch a tree memorize

Now the tree keeps asking. Each colored rectangle is a leaf (a final box); its color is the tree's predicted chance of cancellation there. Slide max_depth up and watch two scores: the practice test (the 800 bookings it learned from) and the real exam (800 it has never seen).

Two brakes: max_depth limits how many questions; min_samples_leaf says "never write a rule from fewer than N guests". Set depth to 15, then raise min_samples_leaf to about 30 and watch the exam score recover. Scores are AUC: 0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect ranking.
STATION 3

Plant a forest: the crowd gets wise

Every tree here is a memorizer: no depth limit at all. But each one learns from a different bootstrap sample (bookings drawn from a hat, each put back), and at every question it may only look at one random clue. Alone, each tree is a wild patchwork. Add trees and watch their average.

Why it works: each tree's mistakes come from the luck of its sample, so they point in different directions and cancel in the average, like 787 fairgoers guessing an ox's weight (Galton, 1906). Adding trees never makes the forest memorize more. It just makes the vote steadier.
STATION 4

Shuffle a clue: which ones does the forest really need?

A forest of 100 trees now uses seven clues. One of them is a trap: booking ID, a made-up number that cannot predict anything. The left bars show the forest's built-in impurity importance. Now press shuffle: we scramble that column on the exam bookings and measure how much worse the forest gets. That's permutation importance. Which clue fools which method?

Why impurity gets fooled: a column where every booking has a different value offers hundreds of places to cut. Memorizing trees always find some cut that looks useful on the training bookings, by pure luck. Shuffling on new bookings asks the honest question: "if this clue were scrambled, would the model notice?" Report permutation importance to your manager.
Data: 1,600 bookings sampled from the course file (Antonio, de Almeida & Nunes, 2019), the training data of our Kaggle competition; lead time ≤ 400 days, price per night €1–300. Trees use Gini and greedy binary splits, like scikit-learn's DecisionTreeClassifier; forests use bootstrap samples and random clues at every split.
